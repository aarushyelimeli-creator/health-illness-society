Who Gets Counted: Chlamydia in the United States, 1970–2020

How chlamydia went from something the United States did not track at all to the most-reported disease in the country, and why both the numbers and the fallout landed unevenly across race, age, and income.

Time Period
1970–2020
Geography
United States
Researcher
Aarush Yelimeli
About This Project

A Fifty-Year Look at Who Gets Tested, Treated, and Counted

This site follows chlamydia in the United States across fifty years, starting from a period when the infection was not tracked at all and ending at its place today as the most frequently reported condition in the country, and the timing of all of that matters, because in 1970 there was no national surveillance system for chlamydia whatsoever, data collection did not even begin until 1984, the infection was not made nationally notifiable until 1995, and it was not reported from all fifty states until 2000, which means the rising case numbers over this period are really recording two things at the same time, one of them being a spreading infection and the other being a health system that only slowly started looking for it.

Reported rates are highest among young women, and among Black women in particular, which reflects real differences in infection along with even bigger differences in who gets screened and where, and what this project sets out to figure out is how much of that disparity is actually disease and how much of it is just the condition of the health system itself.

Key Themes

Three themes run through this project, the first being who gets counted and why surveillance data is never neutral, the second being how race, age, and geography shape a person's access to testing and treatment, and the third being how stigma and the gendered design of screening programs put most of the work of prevention onto young women.

What the Research Showed

What stands out the most is how much these numbers depend on the healthcare system around them, because a jump in reported cases does not always mean the infection suddenly became more common, since sometimes it just means more people were tested, or a state started reporting for the first time, and the gaps between racial and age groups stand out too, because chlamydia is obviously a biological infection, but who gets tested and who can afford care are social questions, and those are the things that end up deciding what shows up in the data.

Chronology

Timeline

1988
The Region X Infertility Prevention Project Begins

The first large, organized chlamydia screening program launched in family planning clinics across Alaska, Idaho, Oregon, and Washington, where the clinics tested young women and treated the ones who came back positive, and this is really the reason disparities in chlamydia became visible at all, because the program produced the first solid body of data on who was actually infected, and from 1988 through the mid-1990s positivity among clients aged 15 to 24 fell by more than 60%, but nevertheless the racial gap widened instead of closing, since in 1988 positivity among Black clients was 62% higher than among white clients, 16.4% against 10.1%, and by 1996 that difference had grown all the way to 100%.

1995
Chlamydia Becomes Nationally Notifiable

After national data collection began in 1984, chlamydia was formally made a nationally notifiable condition in 1995, which required cases to be reported to federal surveillance, so for the first time the country had a way of knowing how common the infection actually was, and in that very first year chlamydia was already the most commonly reported infectious disease in the United States.

2000
All Fifty States Finally Reporting

Chlamydia was not reported from all fifty states and the District of Columbia until 2000, which was five years after it became notifiable and thirty years into this study's window, and the CDC received more than 700,000 reports that year, though much of the apparent increase in cases through the 1990s is really just states joining the surveillance system rather than the infection spreading that quickly.

2001
The USPSTF Recommends Screening for Women

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommended that clinicians screen all sexually active women at increased risk for chlamydia, then updated that guidance in 2007 to cover every sexually active nonpregnant woman aged 24 or younger, while men were largely left out, because the Task Force's own evidence review admitted there were large gaps in what was known about screening them and it could not say whether screening men helped or not, which makes a national prevention strategy aimed almost entirely at young women a gender story as much as a clinical one, since untested men stay a reservoir of infection while women carry the testing and the stigma that comes with it.

2020
COVID-19 and the Cases That Disappeared

During the week of April 8, 2020, reported chlamydia cases fell to roughly half of what they had been in the same week of 2019, and cumulative totals for the year finished 14.0% below 2019, but infections did not drop by 14% in a single year, the testing did, as clinics closed and people stayed away from care, and this is the cleanest evidence in the whole timeline that these numbers measure health system access as much as they measure disease.

Event Analysis

2020: COVID-19 and the Cases That Disappeared

Background

By early 2020, chlamydia was the most frequently reported condition in the United States, backed by three decades of data coming out of family planning clinics, STI clinics, student health centers, and primary care offices, but that data depended almost entirely on people physically showing up for care, and a lot of it was opportunistic testing done during visits that had been scheduled for something else entirely, so when the pandemic closed clinics and redirected public health staff to COVID work, the testing itself collapsed.

Significance

Reported cases dropped to about half of the previous year's level within weeks, and the cumulative total for 2020 finished 14.0% below 2019, and there is honestly no way that many infections actually disappeared in a few months, so what fell was detection, and that single year showed that the national chlamydia case count is really a measure of testing capacity layered on top of disease, while the people who lost access first were the same people who unfortunately had always depended the most on public clinics for their care.

“
case rates are heavily influenced by screening coverage
— According to CDC, Sexually Transmitted Disease Surveillance 2020, 2022
Societal Implications

The people who depended the most on free and low-cost public clinics were younger, poorer, and more often Black and Latina, and they were the first ones to lose access to testing and they lost the most of it, while people with private insurance or the option of a video appointment kept more of theirs, and an infection nobody finds is an infection nobody treats, so untreated chlamydia can do real damage, including pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy (a pregnancy that grows outside the uterus), and infertility, and this is the part that should worry people the most, because fewer reported cases in 2020 could turn into more women getting hurt years later, in the same communities that were already getting the worst care.

“
an already strained public health infrastructure
— Jonathan Mermin, CDC, quoted in the 2020 STD Surveillance Report press release, 2022
Conclusion

What 2020 compressed into a single year is the same pattern this site traces across fifty, because what the country knows about chlamydia has always depended on who we chose to test, which means the curve from 1970 to 2020 is the history of an infection and the history of a health system's disparities at the same time, and it is honestly hard to read one without reading the other.

Source Synthesis · Topic 1

Who Gets Counted: Surveillance and the Making of a Statistic

The infrastructure came before the numbers did, since national data collection did not start until 1984, chlamydia was not made notifiable until 1995, and complete state reporting did not happen until 2000, and reported rates climbed steeply across that whole stretch, so it would be very easy to look at that climb and assume the infection was spreading that fast, but a lot of what is actually climbing is the surveillance system itself, and Hoover and her team are the most useful source on this, because they pulled from several different data sources for the specific reason that no single one of them gives a clean picture of screening among young women, and even their best estimate was that fewer than half of sexually active females aged 15 to 21 had been tested in the past year, while at a regular doctor's office visit a chlamydia test only happened 4.3% of the time.

“
fewer than half of sexually active females aged 15–21 years screened annually
— Hoover et al., MMWR Supplements, 2014

So for every young woman who shows up in the national case count, there are others who were never tested at all and therefore never counted, which means the number is not wrong, exactly, it is just measuring something much narrower than what most people assume it is measuring, and nevertheless it still gets treated as if it were a headcount of infections.

If rates track screening, then groups who get screened in publicly funded settings like family planning clinics, STI clinics, and Medicaid are going to show up in the data far more often than groups who are never tested in the first place, and it is worth being careful here, because this does not mean the disparity is fake, since prevalence studies find it too, but it does mean the size of the gap in reported cases and the size of the gap in actual infection are not the same number, and a careful reader has to hold onto both of those ideas at once, which the CDC has actually admitted about its own data.

“
the number of STD cases reported to CDC undercounts the actual number of infections
— CDC, National Center for Health Statistics, 2023

In other words, the agency producing the statistic is openly telling you that the statistic is low, and yet those same reported counts are what end up in news headlines and in arguments about which communities have a problem, and unfortunately the warning almost never travels with the number.

Source Synthesis · Topic 2

Race, Age, and the Geography of Care

Chambers and her co-authors do the most work here, because studying women in King County, Washington, they estimated that among those born between 1980 and 1984 the lifetime risk of being diagnosed with chlamydia was 14.0% for non-Hispanic white women, 32.6% for Hispanic women, and 64.9% for non-Hispanic Black women, and what makes their study so useful is that it is a lifetime-risk study with reproductive outcomes attached to it, so a diagnosis rate connects to an actual consequence instead of sitting there as an abstract percentage, and age only makes it worse, since in the CDC screening data only about a quarter of 15- to 17-year-olds reported a test in the past year, compared to half of 20- to 21-year-olds.

“
a risk almost 5 times that in non-Hispanic whites
— Chambers et al., Clinical Infectious Diseases, 2018

What that shows is that two women the same age, living in the same county, walking into clinics a few miles apart, were facing risks that were not even close to comparable, and a gap that large inside one county in one state is striking, because it makes it very hard to argue that this is about anything other than how care is distributed.

Now pair that with what happened in Region X, where the screening program worked, and it worked well, since positivity among young clients fell by more than 60% from 1988 through the mid-1990s, and yet the racial gap widened instead of closing, going from 62% in 1988 all the way to 100% by 1996, and Fine and his co-authors then adjusted for individual risk factors and for neighborhood-level income, and the racial differences still did not disappear.

“
Racial/ethnic differences in chlamydia persisted after adjusting for individual and aggregate factors.
— Fine et al., Public Health Reports, 2012

This means a program can succeed and fail at the same time, which is honestly the most interesting finding in any of these sources, because everybody got better off while the people who were worst off stayed the furthest behind, and a program that worked overall and still left a gap that wide is a much harder problem to think about than a program that simply did not work at all.

So what actually explains it? The causes these sources point toward are structural ones, like how close a clinic is to where somebody lives, whether they have insurance, whether they can get transportation to an appointment, and how intensely different groups get screened in the first place, and it is important to avoid explanations that come down to individual behavior, because the sources do not support that reading and the argument is stronger without it, and the King County data even shows the gap narrowing over time, since among non-Hispanic Black women the risk of diagnosis by age 24 fell from 57.3% in the 1980–1984 birth cohort to 38.6% in the 1990–1994 cohort, which shows that these gaps were never fixed facts about people, because the things that ended up changing were things that can be changed.

Source Synthesis · Topic 3

Stigma, Sex Education, and the Gendered Burden of Prevention

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force is where this starts, because in 2001 it recommended screening sexually active women at increased risk, and by 2007 it had extended that to every sexually active nonpregnant woman aged 24 or younger, while for men it could not reach a conclusion at all, and its own evidence review said plainly that the research on screening men was full of holes.

“
large gaps in the evidence about screening men
— Meyers et al., evidence update for the USPSTF, 2007

This shows that the country built a national prevention strategy while openly admitting it did not know what half the population needed, and if you follow what comes out of that decision, women get tested at reproductive health visits, men largely do not get tested anywhere, so women show up in the case data and men mostly do not, which means the disparity in the numbers is partly just a disparity in who somebody decided to look at.

Now layer stigma and sex education on top of that, because most chlamydia infections produce no symptoms, so an infection only gets found if somebody goes looking for it, which means a teenager has to bring up STI testing with a provider on purpose, and abstinence-focused sex education makes that conversation much harder, since a student who was never taught that asymptomatic infections exist has no reason to ask for a test in the first place, and meanwhile the work of asking for a test and being diagnosed sits with young women, and so does the shame, which echoes how the burden always seems to land on whoever is already being watched the closest, so where exactly is the line between a prevention program and a program that just decided one group was easier to monitor?

“
a fraction of America's STD epidemic
— CDC, quoted in Brian Pascus, CBS News, 2019

Even the CDC, then, calls its own record-high numbers a partial picture, and most people reading a headline about record STD rates would probably assume it is a story about behavior getting worse, and further than that they might assume something about the people in those communities, when what the headline is actually reporting is a system that got better at looking in some places and never really looked in others.
